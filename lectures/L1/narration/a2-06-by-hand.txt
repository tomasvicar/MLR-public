This is the size of example that comes up in the labs and in the practical test: six points, a table of distances, and a decision. The practical test asks for the distance and the k nearest neighbours decision. The k-means step on the right is self-study material. The six training points in the line at the top are the data of the k nearest neighbours code example, three of class A and three of class B.

Start with the left table. The new sample is the point two, three, and k is three. Let me compute one distance out loud, to the point one point five, one point eight. The differences are zero point five and one point two. Their squares are zero point two five and one point four four. Add them, take the square root, and you get one point three oh. Now check the other rows yourself in the same way. The table is sorted from the nearest point to the farthest, and the three bold distances are the three nearest. All three are class A, so the vote is unanimous: class A. Notice the remark in brackets. Even with k equal to five the vote is three to two, still A.

Now the right table, one iteration of Lloyd's algorithm with k equal to two. The means are initialised to two of the samples, mu one at the point one, two, and mu two at the point five, eight. For each point the table gives its distance to mu one and to mu two, and the cluster column is simply the nearer mean. That puts the three class A points into cluster one and the three class B points into cluster two.

For this assignment the inertia J is thirty-six point two five. You can check it from the table: square each point's distance to its own mean and add them up. Then comes the update. Each new mean is the average of the points in its cluster, coordinate by coordinate. For cluster one, the first coordinates one, one point five and one average to one point one seven. The second coordinates two, one point eight and zero point six average to one point four seven. Cluster two gives seven point three three and nine point oh oh. With the new means, J drops from thirty-six point two five to fifteen point nine eight.

The last thing to point out: after the update the assignment does not change. Every point stays in its cluster, and that is the stopping condition, so the algorithm has converged. The numbers on this slide are checked by the figure generator against the code example.
