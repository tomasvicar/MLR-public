A random variable is just a number produced by a random experiment. How you describe which numbers are likely depends on whether the values can be counted.

The left panel is the discrete case, a fair die. Six faces, each with probability one sixth. This is a probability mass function, a PMF: every bar is a probability, none is negative, and together they sum to one.

The right panel is the continuous case, a model of body height: a normal density centred at one hundred seventy-five centimetres, with a standard deviation of seven. Here is the most common confusion. The height of this curve is not a probability. Only the area under it is. The shaded band says that a height from this model falls between one hundred seventy and one hundred eighty centimetres with probability zero point five two five.

Two consequences follow, and both surprise people. First, the probability of exactly one hundred seventy-five point zero zero zero, with the zeros going on forever, is zero. A single point has no width, so it has no area. Second, a density may be larger than one. This curve stays below zero point zero six, but a normal distribution with sigma equal to zero point one peaks at three point nine nine. Its area is still one, it is just very tall and very thin.

Where does this come back? The softmax of the previous slide produces a PMF over the classes. The output of every classifier in this course is a discrete distribution like the die, one bar per class. A generative model, on the other hand, learns a density and then samples from it.
