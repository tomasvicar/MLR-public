A linear function is defined by the two properties in the first bullet on the left. Additivity: the image of a sum is the sum of the images. Homogeneity: scale the input by a, and the output scales by a. Together they are the superposition principle, the same definition as in the linearity recap of the third lecture.

Now the three panels. The light grey grid is the input, a square grid around the origin; the teal grid is where the function sends it.

The left panel is the linear map. The red dot at the origin stays where it was. The grid lines stay straight, stay parallel and stay evenly spaced. A linear map can rotate the grid, stretch it, shear it or flip it, and that is all it can do. Why must the origin stay? Every linear function from d dimensions to m dimensions is a matrix times x, and a matrix times the zero vector is the zero vector.

The middle panel is the affine map. It is the same grid, only moved by the vector b, the red arrow. The lines are still straight and parallel; what has changed is that the origin has left. That is exactly W x plus b, linear plus a shift.

In the right panel the lines bend. That map is neither linear nor affine.

The one-dimensional example on the right shows that a shift really breaks linearity. Take f of x equals two x plus one. f of one plus one is f of two, which is five. But f of one plus f of one is three plus three, which is six. Five is not six, so the function is not linear.

Here is the naming trap. In machine learning, linear regression and a linear layer are really affine, because of the bias. Mathematically, the bias breaks linearity, but nobody renames them, so you have to know what is meant.

The last bullet on the right is the reason a neural network needs nonlinear activations. An affine map of an affine map is again affine. Multiply W two into the bracket, W one x plus b one, and add b two. You get W two W one times x, plus a new shift, W two b one plus b two. One matrix and one shift. So without a nonlinear activation, any number of stacked layers collapses into a single affine map. That is why the seventh lecture adds the activations.
