These rules look new, but every row of the table is a one-dimensional rule in disguise. The first row, a transpose w, is the vector version of a times w, whose derivative is a. The second, the squared length of w, is w squared, whose derivative is two w. The third, w transpose A w with a symmetric A, is a times w squared, whose derivative is two a w. If you forget a row, write down the one-dimensional version and it comes back.

The last row is the one least squares needs. It is the squared error of a linear model over all n samples. Its gradient is the chain rule of the previous slide, done for all n samples at once: two times the residual, carried back through X. With a single feature it reads two times the sum over i of x i times the bracket, w x i minus y i.

Under the table is the shape check. X transpose is d by n, the residual X w minus y is n by one, so the product is d by one, the shape of w. That is the fastest way to catch a wrong transpose: a gradient always has the shape of whatever you differentiate with respect to. Forget the transpose, and you get n by d times n by one, which does not even fit.

Now the three lines on the right. They are the complete least-squares method. First, set the gradient to zero. Divide by two and move X transpose y to the other side, and you get the second line, X transpose X times w equals X transpose y, the normal equations. Then solve, which gives the third line: w star is the inverse of X transpose X, times X transpose y.

Ridge adds lambda times the squared length of w to the loss. By the second row of the table, that adds two lambda w to the gradient, and after the same steps the only change is lambda I next to X transpose X. In other words, ridge only adds lambda to the diagonal of X transpose X, and that also makes it invertible.

You will meet all of this again in the fifth lecture. It derives OLS by exactly these steps, and its ridge solution is the inverse of X transpose X plus lambda I, times X transpose y.
