Three conventions run through the whole course. A bold lowercase letter is a vector, an uppercase letter is a matrix, and a single component or a scalar is in plain type. So bold x is one sample, capital X is the whole dataset, and x with the subscript j is one number inside the vector.

The table has three columns. The middle one is the definition, the right one shows where the symbol turns up in the course. If a definition says nothing to you, read the right column first.

Notice that a vector is always a column. That is why one sample, written as a row of the data matrix, appears as x i transpose, in the fourth row of the table.

Look carefully at the row with min and argmin, because it is a recap-quiz question. Min gives the smallest value of the function. Argmin gives the point where that value is attained. The lowest loss is a value; the trained weights are the point.

Books write some of this differently. The i-th sample is often x with a superscript i in parentheses, and the number of samples is capital N. The meaning is the same.

And here is the trap. Some symbols are overloaded, and the context decides. On the probability slides an uppercase letter is a random variable, and A and B are events, not matrices. Sigma is a standard deviation, and also the sigmoid. Lambda is an eigenvalue, and also the ridge penalty.
